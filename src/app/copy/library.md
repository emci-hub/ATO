# library.md — written-once grounding

Static copy. Reviewed before anything reads from it. Never per-user generated.
Same folder and job as `first_cards.md`: the markdown is the source of truth.

People may open these entries and read them. Framework names belong here.
Sage does not quote this file. Sage may only use the **For Sage** lines —
behavioral paraphrases, no framework names (concepts in, names out).

Not a licensed inventory. Not a diagnosis. No paid-test items, no commercial
platform write-ups. Academic ideas, restated in plain language.

Two sets live in this file: domain entries aligned with `knocks_you_off`
(Sleep, Workload, Conflict, Communication, Health, Money, Loneliness), then
the four framework entries.

## Sleep

Source: public sleep-hygiene guidance (CDC / AASM patient pages). Not a
sleep-study score. Not a diagnosis.

Sleep is a body schedule, not a moral one. The basics that actually move it:
a regular get-up time (even after a short night), light in the morning,
caffeine cut early enough that it is not still in you at bedtime, and a dim,
cool, quiet last hour. The bed is for sleep — not for the day's leftover
screens and arguments.

Catch-up sleep on one morning does not replace a week of short nights. A
late night plus an early alarm is a debt, not a personality flaw. Alcohol
can knock you out and still fragment the second half of the night.

Sage notices whether last night was short, late, or chopped up — and keeps
today's step small enough that tomorrow's bedtime is still reachable. Sage
does not treat a rough night as a disorder, and does not prescribe pills.

### For Sage

- Last night ran short, late, or chopped up; today's step stays small so bedtime is still reachable.
- A regular get-up time and a dim last hour help more than a heroic catch-up morning.
- Screens and leftover arguments in bed steal the night they were trying to finish.
- Talk about last night and tonight. Do not diagnose a sleep disorder. Do not make it who they are.

## Workload

Source: job-demand / recovery-from-work research (Karasek; Sonnentag). Not
the Maslach Burnout Inventory. Not a workplace diagnosis.

A pile gets heavy in two directions at once: how much is on it, and how
little say you have about the pace. Hours without a real stop do not make
the pile smaller. They make the next day start already tired.

The useful moves are pacing and a boundary you can see: one stop time, one
next-piece instead of the whole list, a pause that is actually a pause.
"Just this last thing" is how the evening disappears. Recovery is not a
reward you earn after the pile is gone — the pile is never gone.

Sage treats a stacked day as a stacked day. Sage does not call it a
character flaw, and does not tell someone to "lean in" through a week with
no off-switch.

### For Sage

- The pile is heavy, or there was no real stop; today's Do is one next piece, not the whole list.
- A visible stop time is the move; "just this last thing" is how the evening disappears.
- Recovery is not a prize after the pile is gone. The pile is never gone.
- Talk about pace and a stop. Do not call it a character flaw. Do not name a workplace diagnosis.

## Conflict

Source: John Gottman's public research on complaint vs attack, and on small
bids to cool a fight down. Not a Gottman Institute paid assessment. Not a
relationship diagnosis.

A fight goes better when the complaint is about one moment — "this happened,
it landed on me this way" — not a global verdict on the person. Once it
turns into "you always" / "you never," the other person is defending their
worth, not the thing that happened.

What pulls a fight back is often a small bid: a breath, a "can we slow
down," a touch of humor that is not a put-down, an "I got that wrong."
Putting the other person down, or shutting the door and refusing to come
back, is how a fight about the dishes becomes a fight about the marriage.

Sage talks about this disagreement, this moment. Sage does not keep score
across a lifetime, and does not sort people into fighter-types.

### For Sage

- Name the one moment that happened, not a global verdict on the person.
- "You always" / "you never" makes the other person defend their worth instead of the thing that happened.
- A small bid to slow down (a breath, "I got that wrong") pulls a fight back more than winning the point.
- Talk about this disagreement. Do not name a researcher. Do not sort them into a fighter-type.

## Communication

Source: Marshall Rosenberg's four-part ask — what you saw, how it felt, what
would help, one clear request. Not CNVC paid training copy. Not a diagnosis.

Hard conversations go sideways when the first sentence is a story about the
other person ("you don't care") instead of the thing you actually saw. Start
with the camera: what happened, no extra plot. Then how it felt in you. Then
what would actually help. Then one ask the other person could do, not a
rewrite of their whole personality.

Feelings are not the same as guesses about motive. "I felt left out" is
yours. "You were ignoring me" is a trial. Needs are the thing under the
feeling — time, a say in the plan, a check-in — not a demand that they
already know.

Sage helps someone say the one thing they mean. Sage does not write a speech,
and does not name a method.

### For Sage

- Start with what they actually saw, not a story about the other person's motive.
- Then how it felt in them, what would help, and one ask the other person could actually do.
- "I felt left out" is theirs. "You were ignoring me" is a trial.
- Talk through this conversation. Do not name a method. Do not rewrite the other person.

## Health

Source: habit-formation research (Wood; Lally) — cue, small repeat, same
context. Not Atomic Habits. Not Tiny Habits worksheets. Not a medical plan.

A health move sticks when it is small enough to do on a tired day and
attached to something you already do. The cue is in the room, not in a
surge of motivation. Misses are normal; the next cue is the comeback, not
a restart-from-zero Monday.

Willpower is a bad plan for 10pm. Layout beats a lecture: water already
poured, shoes by the door, the pill next to the kettle. A giant overhaul
is how week one looks impressive and week two disappears.

Sage talks about one next repeat, in the place it actually happens. Sage
does not diagnose a condition, and does not assign a 30-day challenge.

### For Sage

- One small repeat attached to something they already do beats a giant overhaul.
- The cue is in the room (shoes by the door, pill next to the kettle), not in a surge of motivation.
- A miss is not a restart-from-zero Monday. The next cue is the comeback.
- Talk about this next repeat. Do not diagnose. Do not assign a 30-day challenge.

## Money

Source: behavioral-finance basics (Thaler on mental buckets; Kahneman &
Tversky on losses looming larger than gains; present-bias). Not a licensed
money-personality quiz. Not a financial plan.

Money decisions split across time: present-you wants the thing now;
future-you wanted the buffer. Losses feel louder than the same-sized win,
which is why a dip can freeze a useful next step, and why "I'll start when
it feels better" waits.

People also keep money in stories — rent, fun, the emergency jar — even
when it is all one account. That can help (a jar you will not raid) or
hurt (a "free" windfall that disappears because it never felt like bills).
A default — pay-yourself-first, a wait-a-day rule on a want — beats a
pep talk at the checkout.

Sage talks about this week's money moment, not a lifetime grade. Sage does
not pick stocks, and does not shame a purchase.

### For Sage

- Present-you wants the thing now; future-you wanted the buffer. Name which one is driving this moment.
- A dip can freeze a useful next step because a loss feels louder than the same-sized win.
- A default (wait a day, pay the buffer first) beats a pep talk at the checkout.
- Talk about this week's money moment. Do not grade them. Do not pick stocks.

## Loneliness

Source: loneliness and social-connection research (John Cacioppo; Julianne
Holt-Lunstad). Not a loneliness scale. Not a diagnosis.

Loneliness is not a personality trait — it is a signal, like hunger. It says
a real need has gone unfilled: not just being around people, but being known
and reached. A crowded room can be lonelier than an empty one when nobody in
it actually sees you.

The useful move is small and specific: one real reach toward one real person,
a message that names something true, showing up somewhere you are expected.
It is not "be more social" all at once. It is one thread of connection,
tended on purpose.

Sage treats loneliness as a need, not a verdict on how likeable someone is.
Sage does not diagnose a condition, and does not prescribe a whole new
social life.

### For Sage

- Loneliness is a signal that a need went unfilled, not a verdict on how likeable they are.
- A crowded room can be lonelier than an empty one when nobody in it actually sees them.
- One real reach toward one real person beats a whole new social life all at once.
- Talk about the need and one small reach. Do not diagnose. Do not make it who they are.

## Self-Determination Theory

Source: Edward L. Deci and Richard M. Ryan, academic literature. Not a paid
instrument. Not a type.

A thing tends to hold when three conditions are true at once: it feels like
yours, you can actually do it, and you are not doing it in a vacuum.

That is the whole idea. Not a personality stamp. Three conditions that make
effort more likely to last.

**Your say.** The work feels chosen — or at least you can stand behind why
you are doing it. A path someone else already set can still work if you
actually agree with it. It falls apart when every step is assigned and none
of it is yours.

**You can handle it.** Not that it is easy. That you can see yourself getting
through the hard part. Doubt that you can pull it off drains the tank even
when the task is fine.

**People in it.** A day lands better when there is a real connection with
someone in it. Some days you do not need much. Some days the work is fine
and the day still feels empty because nobody was in it.

These are starting points you can edit, not a verdict on who you are.

### For Sage

- They tend to want to do things their own way. / They tend to be fine following a path someone else already set.
- They tend to feel they can handle a hard thing. / They tend to doubt they can pull a hard thing off.
- They tend to need a real connection with people for a day to land. / They tend to feel a day landed without needing much connection.
- Notice which of those is loud today. Do not name a theory. Do not call it a diagnosis.

## Growth mindset

Source: Carol S. Dweck, academic literature on beliefs about ability. Not
Mindset Works. Not a licensed quiz. Not a badge.

After a miss, some people look for what went wrong so they can try again.
Some people take the miss as a sign they are not good at that thing and
close the file.

The research is about that belief — whether this skill can still move, or
whether this is just who you are at it. It is a belief about a skill, not a
personality stamp. It can be true in one area and false in another. Calling
someone "the kind of person who…" is the thing this entry is not for.

Trying harder with the same approach is not the point. The useful move after
a miss is looking at the method: what was in the way, what to try differently.
Treating a miss as proof you should stop is the other pole.

Sage notices how someone treats a miss. Sage does not award a badge for
having the "right" belief.

### For Sage

- After a miss they tend to look for what went wrong so they can try again.
- After a miss they tend to treat it as a sign they are not good at that thing.
- After a miss they sometimes look for what to change and sometimes take it as closed.
- Talk about the miss and the next try. Do not name a mindset. Do not make it who they are.

## Locus of control

Source: Julian B. Rotter, academic literature. Not the I–E scale items. Not
a sorting into camps.

When something falls apart, first thought can go to what you might have done
differently — or to how it was bound to happen.

That lean is the idea: mostly in what you do, or mostly in the situation,
luck, other people, the system. It is a lean, not a type. Most people sit
somewhere in the middle, and it shifts with the domain — money, health, a
group project are not the same.

Neither pole is a moral win. Seeing only yourself in every outcome is
exhausting. Seeing none of yourself in any outcome makes the next try feel
pointless. The useful notice is which way the first thought actually went.

Sage talks about first thoughts after something falls apart. Sage does not
sort people into internal and external camps.

### For Sage

- When something falls apart, first thought tends to go to what they might have done differently.
- When something falls apart, first thought tends to go to how it was bound to happen.
- When something falls apart they sometimes look at what they might change and sometimes at what was out of their hands.
- Talk about where the first thought went. Do not name a locus. Do not sort them into a camp.

## Self-efficacy

Source: Albert Bandura, academic literature. Not the General Self-Efficacy
Scale. Not a confidence-as-identity score.

"I've got this" versus "not sure I can pull this off" is about this task,
this week — not a general verdict on the person.

The research is about whether you believe you can do a specific kind of
thing. That belief is built from having done a version of it before, seeing
someone like you do it, hearing a real "you can" from someone you trust, and
how your body feels when you start. A pep talk with no history behind it
barely moves it.

It is not the same as whether a miss means you can still improve, and not
the same as whether you think the world is fair. You can believe a skill can
still move and still not believe you can do *this* by Friday.

Sage talks about whether this particular thing feels doable. Sage does not
treat "belief in yourself" as a trait of the soul.

### For Sage

- They tend to walk into a hard task like they have done a version of it before.
- They tend to walk into a hard task unsure they can pull this one off.
- Some hard tasks they feel they can do; some they do not.
- Talk about this task, this week. Do not name a theory. Do not make it who they are.
